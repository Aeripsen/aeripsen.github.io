Four protocols, one detector, and the command no one asked for

The attack worth catching on a smart lock or an infusion pump is not malformed traffic. It is a perfectly well formed command that no human asked for: valid bytes, valid protocol, no legitimate trigger anywhere behind it. I am building an intrusion detection system that hunts that absence across BLE, Zigbee, Z-Wave and Wi-Fi at once, on a live testbed of real devices, because a single room runs all four and an intruder only has to be fluent in one.

Status, stated plainly

This is design, a working capture rig, real captured attack data, and a roadmap. It is not a finished, benchmarked product, and I will not describe it as one until the numbers exist. What is real today: the testbed, the three way capture, labelled attack captures across multiple families on live hardware, the taxonomy, and the detector design. What is not: trained and scored detection models across all four protocols.

The gap

Every public IoT security dataset I can find labels traffic from the outside. You get a packet, a timestamp, a size, and a label that says "attack" because the person generating it knew they were attacking. Nothing in the record says what the device was actually told to do.

A detector trained on those labels learns the noise attacks make, not the act. It can flag a flood and stay blind while a lock receives a clean, well formed unlock. You cannot see the difference from packet shape alone, and you cannot even label it correctly without decoding what the command said. Fixing the label is where this project starts.

The build

  • A three tap capture rig

    Every event is recorded three ways at once: raw over the air RF, hub side logs, and the IP traffic leaving the network. One event, three independent views, which is what lets a label be checked rather than asserted. It is also what proves an attack landed: in one capture an injected command was independently recovered off the air, separately from the tooling that sent it.

  • Decoded application semantic labelling

    The core novelty. Labels are written from the decoded application layer of each protocol, so a record carries the actual command that was issued, not just the bytes that carried it. No released public dataset I know of carries this. It is the difference between "unusual traffic to the lock" and "the lock was told to unlock".

  • A 15 family cross protocol attack taxonomy

    Fifteen attack families organised so the same family can be recognised across protocols that share nothing at the wire level. The taxonomy carries an explicit falsifiable merge test: a stated rule for when two families are really one family, so the count is an argument rather than a preference. Figure generation checks itself against the taxonomy, so a diagram cannot quietly drift out of sync with the data.

  • A two tier detector

    Tier one runs on cheap wire level features and has to be fast enough to be real time. Tier two adds the provenance signal: a command that arrived with no legitimate device trigger behind it. Tier one catches the noisy attacks. Tier two is what catches the quiet, well formed ones, and it is only possible because of the decoded labelling above.

  • A leak free cross protocol transfer test

    Train on two protocols, test on a third the model has never seen. If a detector has learned something about attack behaviour rather than about one radio's quirks, this is where it shows. It is designed as the falsification test for the whole premise, which is why it is in the plan before the results are in.

Roadmap

What is being trained and scored now.

Model families are chosen and cited, matched to what each attack class actually looks like rather than picked for being fashionable. First scored evaluations are running.

Beyond that, a retrieval layer over flow, device state, packet and metadata for explainable triage: an analyst should be able to ask why an alert fired and get the evidence rather than a score. That one is on the roadmap, not built.

Scope and care

Every attack in this work is generated against hardware I own or my lab owns, on an isolated testbed, for the purpose of producing labelled defensive training data. The taxonomy and the per family implementation matrix exist so the work is reproducible inside a lab. They are not published here, and specific tooling, commands and radio configurations stay out of public writeups on purpose.

The work runs as a collaboration, including with a research institute abroad. Credit for it is shared accordingly.
