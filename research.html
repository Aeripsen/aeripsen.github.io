Hierarchical UDP and QUIC intrusion detection, with a reject option

First author. Accepted to SECRYPT 2026, the International Conference on Security and Cryptography. The paper pairs a two stage detector that is allowed to say "I do not know" with a new labelled dataset of 1.22 million UDP and QUIC flows, which the lab released publicly under CC BY 4.0.

The problem

QUIC moved a large share of the web onto UDP and encrypted almost everything a middlebox used to read. That is good for users and awkward for defenders: the fields intrusion detection used to key on are gone, so detection has to work from the shape of the traffic rather than its contents. Volumetric attacks hide inside that shape well.

The second problem is the data. Public intrusion datasets are old, mostly TCP, and rarely carry QUIC at all. So the work had to produce the measurement instrument and the dataset before it could produce a result.

What is in it

  • A two stage detector with an escalation threshold

    A cheap first stage scores a flow on UDP only features. When it is not confident enough, the flow escalates to a second stage that also sees QUIC derived features. The threshold is a real control, not a hyperparameter: it moves how much of the traffic the system answers for against how often that answer holds up.

  • A reject option, reported as a curve

    The honest way to report an imbalanced detector. Instead of one accuracy number, the system reports coverage against reliability across thresholds, so an operator can pick the operating point their alert budget actually allows. The interactive version of that curve is on the front page.

  • BCCC-UDP-QUIC-IDS-2025, a 1.22 million flow labelled dataset

    Generated on a controlled testbed, labelled per flow, and published by the lab under CC BY 4.0 so the results can be checked by someone who does not trust me. Attack families cover several classes of UDP and QUIC flooding and evasion.

    bccc.laps.yorku.ca/BCCC-UDP-QUIC-IDS-2025/ (served over HTTP)

  • Two open source flow feature extractors

    QUICFlowLyzer and UDPFlowLyzer, the tools that turn packet captures into the flow feature vectors everything downstream consumes. They live in my lab's GitHub organisation alongside its other analyzers, and they are the reason I can defend any single feature in the model for as long as someone wants to ask.

    github.com/ahlashkari (Behaviour Centric Cybersecurity Centre)
The hard part

The class imbalance is the whole story.

Real attack traffic is not balanced and pretending otherwise is how intrusion detection papers get to report numbers nobody can reproduce operationally. In the UDP portion of this dataset the largest attack class carries 621,177 flows and the smallest carries 166. That is a ratio of roughly 3,700 to 1.

Detecting that there is an attack on this data is very achievable. Attributing it to the right family, when one family has 3,700 times the evidence of another, is an open problem rather than a bug someone forgot to fix. The reject option is the honest response: the system declines the calls it cannot support instead of guessing and calling the guess a result.

A note on splits

Flows from one connection resemble each other. If a random split puts some of a connection's flows in training and the rest in test, the score that comes out is partly memorisation. My own reimplementation, FlowSentry, therefore splits by connection so no 5-tuple appears in both folds, and reports lower numbers than a random split would give. Different protocols produce different numbers, and comparing across them as if it were a scoreboard is not a comparison.

Where it goes

Two directions came out of this. One is deployment: taking the architecture out of the paper and making it serve requests, which is FlowSentry, and which is where I found out that my own architecture loses to a simpler baseline on the shipped sample.

The other is scope. UDP and QUIC are one protocol family on one link. The system I am architecting now does the same job across BLE, Zigbee, Z-Wave and Wi-Fi at once, on real devices, with labels taken from the decoded application layer instead of the wire.
